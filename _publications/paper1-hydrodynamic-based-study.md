**Abstract**
Hydropower plants release turbine discharge through tailrace channels that reconnect to natural rivers, where concentrated outflow often produces high bed shear stress (BSS) and localized scouring. This study investigates the hydrodynamic interactions and assesses various tailrace configurations to mitigate downstream erosion and enhance riverbed stability. Utilizing the Delft3D-FLOW numerical model, the research simulates 2D depth-averaged flow patterns across six distinct scenarios to identify optimal design strategies. The analysis centers on spatial variations in velocity and BSS distribution to evaluate sediment entrainment risks against a critical shear threshold of 0.15 N/m². Configurations include natural river conditions (S0), unmodified tailrace discharge (S1), and structural interventions such as reduced outlet depth (S2) or thin-dam groyne structures (S3–S5). The results show that introducing tailrace discharge substantially increases the maximum BSS from 1.44 N/m² in S0 to 7.42 N/m² in S1, indicating strong jet-like flow and high turbulence near the outlet. Modified scenarios effectively redistribute flow momentum, decreasing spatial BSS concentration. While localized peaks persist near structural boundaries, the mean BSS decreases significantly from 1.14 N/m² in S2 to 0.91 N/m² in S5, suggesting more tranquil near-bed conditions and a reduced likelihood of sediment mobilization. Spatial shear stress plots further confirm that optimized thin-dam arrangements diffuse energy more uniformly, preventing the development of high-shear regions. These findings provide critical design benchmarks for hydropower outlets, demonstrating that structural optimization can ensure long-term hydraulic stability and promote sustainable river management by minimizing downstream morphological changes
